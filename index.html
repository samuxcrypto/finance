Week 1 · Accounting basics (Chapter 1)

Core ideas

  • Three types of information: financial, managerial, tax.
  • Accounting system does three things: interpret and record transactions, classify similar transactions into reports, summarize and communicate to decision makers.
  • External users: owners, creditors, labor unions, government agencies, suppliers, customers, trade associations, general public. Internal users: board, CEO, CFO, VPs, business unit, plant and store managers, line supervisors.
  • Financial reporting objectives. General: info useful for investment and credit decisions. Also: assess amount, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows. Specific: info about economic resources, claims to them, and changes in both.
  • Management accounting objectives: help achieve goals and missions; help evaluate and reward decision makers.
  • Management accounting is: timely, tied to a specific decision maker, future-oriented, a measure of efficiency and effectiveness, a means to an end.
  • Integrity of information: GAAP, FASB, SEC, internal control structure, audits, legislation.

The six elements

ElementMeaningExamples
AssetsResources owned or controlled with future economic benefit. Current (short-term) or non-current.Cash, receivables, machinery
LiabilitiesObligations to outsiders from past transactions. Current or non-current.Accounts payable, loans, salaries payable
Owner's equityResidual interest: assets minus liabilities.Capital, retained earnings, drawings
RevenuesEarnings from core activities.Sales, service revenue, interest income
ExpensesCosts incurred to generate revenue.Rent, utilities, salary, depreciation

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity   OE = Capital + Revenues − Expenses − Drawings

Confusing terms

  • Unearned revenue: cash received, service not yet delivered. A liability.
  • Deferred expense: paid in advance, not yet used. An asset.
  • Accrued expense: used or incurred, not yet paid. Creates a liability.
  • Contingent liability: may or may not occur, depending on a future event.
Drawings are not on the slides' list of debit/credit rules but appear in the OE formula. They are a debit that reduces equity, and they are never an expense.
Weeks 2–3 · Journal, ledger, T-accounts (JJ's Lawn Care)
  • General journal columns: Date, Description (account titles and explanation), Debit, Credit. Debit account first, credit account indented.
  • Ledger: the entire group of accounts. Account: an individual record of increases and decreases.
  • T-account: debit on the left, credit on the right.
  • Double entry: every transaction has equal debits and credits. Debit balances = credit balances, so A = L + OE always holds.
  • Posting: copying journal entries into the ledger accounts, then finding each ending balance.
  • British ledger format: two-sided T style, Dr and Cr halves each with Date, Particulars, J.F., Amount. American format: one running table with Date, Description, Ref, Debit, Credit, Balance.

Weeks 3–4 · Financial statements
StatementTells youTime frame
Income statementRevenues − expenses = net income (or loss)A period
Balance sheetWhere the enterprise stands: assets, liabilities, owner's equityA specific date
Statement of cash flowsHow cash changedA period
  • Revenues bring positive cash flow, expenses negative, whether in the past, present or future.
  • Owner investments and payments to owners are not on the income statement.
  • Operating: cash effects of revenue and expense transactions. Investing: cash effects of buying and selling assets. Financing: cash effects of dealings with owners and creditors.

Weeks 5–6 · Accruals and deferrals (adjusting entries)
  • Adjusting entries are needed when revenue or expenses affect more than one accounting period.
  • Every adjusting entry changes one income statement account (revenue or expense) and one balance sheet account (asset or liability). Cash is never in an adjusting entry.
TypeBeforeAdjusting entryExamples
1. Asset → expense (deferral)Cash paid first; asset recordedDr Expense / Cr AssetDepreciation, supplies, insurance
2. Liability → revenue (deferral)Cash received first; liability recordedDr Unearned revenue / Cr RevenueAirline tickets, season tickets, advance rent
3. Accrue unpaid expenseExpense used, cash not yet paidDr Expense / Cr PayableInterest, wages, property tax
4. Accrue uncollected revenueRevenue earned, cash not yet receivedDr Receivable / Cr RevenueInterest earned, work done but unbilled

Slide examples worked

Depreciation

  • Systematic allocation of a depreciable asset's cost to expense. It is an estimate.
  • Straight-line = Cost ÷ Estimated useful life
  • Lawn mower: 2,500 ÷ 50 months = 50/month. Truck: 15,000 ÷ 60 months = 250/month.
  • Entry: Dr Depreciation expense / Cr Accumulated depreciation. Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset (credit balance), deducted from the asset on the balance sheet. Mower book value after one month: 2,500 − 50 = 2,450.
  • Methods: straight-line (even), units of production (usage or output), double-declining balance (accelerated), sum-of-the-years' digits (accelerated, remaining life ÷ sum of digits).
  • Income tax: a corporation accrues Dr Income taxes expense / Cr Income taxes payable (slide example 780). It is the final adjusting entry.

Cycle order from the slides

Journalize transactions → post to ledger → prepare trial balance → make end-of-year adjustments → prepare adjusted trial balance.

Income statement side gets what was used or earned this period. Balance sheet side holds what benefits or is owed in future periods. If an exam asks why an entry matters, say it in those terms.
Watch-outs for the exam
  • The Week 5–6 adjusted trial balance slide shows cash 3,925 and a 200 dividend, and the partial balance sheet slide shows capital 8,125. These differ from the Week 2–4 figures (cash 4,125, capital 8,000). Know the Week 2–4 numbers as the base case and treat those two slides as a variation.
  • The slide deck spells it "Debt (Dr.)" in the journal header. It means Debit.
  • Depreciation expense is an income statement item. Accumulated depreciation sits on the balance sheet.
  • Net income and cash change are different numbers. Revenue earned on account raises income, not cash.
  • Equity entries for cash payments: owner withdrawals (drawings) reduce equity but never touch the income statement.
